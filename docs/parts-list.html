CR2032 coin cell—Powers the RTC's backup
Safety — the RTC module charges its battery

The common ZS-042 DS3231 board has a charging circuit that trickle-charges its coin cell. A normal CR2032 isn't rechargeable, and charging one can make it swell or leak. Either fit a rechargeable LIR2032, or remove the charging resistor (marked 201, next to the diode) before fitting a CR2032. The fix takes five minutes with a soldering iron.

Added during review

The RTC and its battery weren't in the original generated plan. A Raspberry Pi has no battery-backed clock of its own — at boot it restores a stale time from disk and only corrects once WiFi and NTP come up. For a device whose whole job is waking you at a specific moment, that's a real failure mode, and the fix costs a few dollars on the I2C bus the build already enables.

Why this driver, and why a sealed box

The speaker gets its own sealed chamber of 0.64 litres, measured from the model. That is small, and it rules out most well-regarded 3" drivers: they either need a bigger box or are too quiet for a 3-watt amplifier. The PC83-4 suits it. In this chamber it gives a Qtc of about 1.0, which means slightly full, warm bass rolling off below roughly 150Hz. That's right for voices and radio music at bedside volume.

The Tang Band W3-2141 is the upgrade: lower distortion and a smoother top end, at about three and a half times the price. But its 92mm frame won't fit a 120mm-tall box with 12mm walls, so choosing it means making the cabinet taller.

A sealed box rather than a port: a port only works tuned to a specific driver with nothing else leaking, and it can chuff audibly. Sealed is simpler and more forgiving, and it's what the divider makes possible.

The amplifier stays at 3.2W. That's well below the driver's rating and nowhere near a Tivoli's 6W, but into an efficient full-range driver it is comfortably loud for a bedroom — and this is a device with a maximum volume cap anyway.

Display and light

PartSpecNote
1.54" e-ink displayWaveshare module, 48×33mm board, 200×200Buy the module (screen on its driver board), not the bare panel. The V2 supports partial refresh.
COB LED strip2700K, 5V, 8mm wide, cut to ~40mmCOB rather than a normal strip: a continuous line of light with no visible dots reflecting off the screen, and it can be cut short.
Logic-level MOSFET moduleAO3400 or D4184 based, ~18×17mmLets the Pi dim the strip from GPIO12 or GPIO13. Not the common IRF520 module: it needs 10V to switch fully, and the Pi only gives 3.3V.
Check the display is legible from bed before ordering

The square panel's active area is only about 27.6mm across, which caps the time digits at roughly 14mm tall. That's comfortable at normal bedside distance and marginal beyond about a metre and a half. Mark 14mm on a ruler, put it on the nightstand, and look at it from where you actually sleep. If it fails that test, the 2.9-inch landscape panel is the fallback — bigger digits, but a shape that fits the baffle less neatly.

Two gotchas worth reading before ordering

The LED strip must be 5V. Listings often read "12V or 5V" — there is no 12V rail anywhere in this build. A 12V strip run from 5V glows dim and patchy rather than failing outright, which makes it a frustrating fault to chase once it's glued inside a hood.

The dimmer cannot use GPIO18 or GPIO19. Those are the pins most online tutorials pick for PWM, and they're consumed here by I2S for the amplifier. Use GPIO12 or GPIO13 instead.

Verify against what actually arrives

  • Measure the driver's frame diameter and mounting depth before cutting the baffle. Cabinet height was set from this — a 3" driver's frame is around 80mm, and the internal height needs real clearance around it, not a millimetre.
  • Measure the display module's PCB and ribbon length yourself before finalising the bezel — module dimensions vary between vendors selling the "same" panel.
  • Check the encoder bushing and button diameters against the 6mm and 7mm drill sizes; panel-mount hardware varies more than listings suggest.
  • Confirm the e-ink panel you order supports partial refresh. Without it, the minute digits flash the whole screen every update.
